1 Introduction

Parag 1: Islands as natural labs and conservation priorities • Islands are natural laboratories for studying the effects of environmental change, because their ecosystems are relatively simple (fewer species, simpler interaction networks, clearer boundaries) • They are also conservation priorities: they host many endemic species and a disproportionate share of recent extinctions • Island biotas are particularly sensitive to change, especially invasive species and habitat change

Oceanic island: laboratories to study evolution and anthropic changes impacts, especially in birds

Oceanic islands, because of their small size and remoteness, often host relatively species-poor communities, yet they are also hotspots of endemism and therefore conservation priority areas (Whittaker et al. 2017). The remoteness and relative simplicity of insular ecosystems likely explain their high sensitivity to anthropic threats such as biological invasions and habitat alteration (Whittaker et al. 2017; Spatz et al. 2017; Russell et Kueffer 2019). Therefore, islands have been at the forefront of research in ecology and evolutionary biology. The relative simplicity of their communities allowed to historically disentangle evolutionary mechanisms (XX Darwin), while it provides a highly relevant context to understand the mechanisms of species responses to current anthropogenic changes. Birds have often been at the forefront of these studies. For example, Darwin’s finches in the Galapagos (Grant et Grant 2008) and Hawaiian honey-creepers in Hawaiian islands (Pratt et al. 2005) are model taxa to study adaptive radiations. In parallel, conservation biologists have repeatedly demonstrated the high sensitivity of native insular bird communities to both biological invasion and habitat alteration (Boyer et Jetz 2014; Spatz et al. 2017; A. M. Rogers et al. 2021).

Parag 2: Known impacts of invasive species and habitat change on native birds

• Birds are a classic focal group for studying threats in insular systems • Invasive species and habitat alteration are some of the most impactful threats on islands, having already driven several species to extinction (climate change likely to play a more and more important role in the near future) • Invasive species affect native species directly, through predation and competition • They also act indirectly, e.g. by altering ecological networks, ecosystem functions or habitats (e.g., invasive plants that change vegetation structur and the landscape) (Meyer et Florence 1996) • In addition to invasive species, direct habitat change, especially urbanisation, also strongly affects native bird communities climate change likely to play a more and more important role in the near future)

Birds, as a data-rich taxon, allow a robust understanding of their ecology and distribution patterns (Hoyo 2020). They are frequent colonizer of island and so, are an adequate group for studying anthropogenic threats in insular systems (Matthews et al. 2022). Habitat change and biological invasion are some of the prevalent anthropogenic threats for island biotas (Leclerc, Villéger, et al. 2020). Habitat alteration, such as urbanization affect native communities and invasive species affect directly and indirectly native birds (Spatz et al. 2017), which are generally key conservation targets.

Invasive species have direct impact on native species, through predation and competition. In French Polynesia islands, declines of endemic monarchs are caused by nets predation from invasive black rats and are worsen by aggression from introduced red-vented bulbuls and common mynas [J.-C. Thibault et al. (2002)](Blanvillain et al. 2003)(M. Thibault, Vidal, et al. 2018a). Moreover, introduced species acts as vectors of diseases, such as avian malaria that accentuated the declines of Hawaiian islands avifauna (Atkinson et al. 2014) and Fatu Hiva monarch in Marquesas islands (Igual i Beltrán et al. 2026). Invasive plants, as Miconia calvescens have strong implication in modification of vegetation structure and diversity, or landscape composition in Tahiti, and may induces indirect effect on other biotic communities [Meyer et Florence (1996)](M. Thibault, Masse, et al. 2018).

Habitat alteration and biological invasion are positively correlated threats [Liu et al. (2023)](Marino et al. 2026). That have driven several species to extinction and threatened others in islands (Matthews et al. 2022) (Steadman 1995). Going forward, climate change is likely to join them as a prevalent threat for the biota of insular systems (Leclerc, Courchamp, et al. 2020; Marino et al. 2026).

Parag 3: Birds and the soundscape; a continental bias

• Birds are also a major focus of bioacoustics and soundscape ecology, and are key contributors to the biophony. • The effects of habitat change, especially urbanisation, on soundscapes have been studied mostly in continental contexts • These effects are much less well established on islands

Within terrestrial soundscapes, birds are with insects, the main contributors to biophony (Phillips et al. 2018). Consequently, they are the most studied taxa in soundscape study (Turlington et al. 2024) and are over-represented in papers investigating bioacoustic (Shannon et al. 2016).

Habitat alteration, especially urbanization emits anthropogenic noise that have acoustic consequences through masking effect for birds (Shannon et al. 2016). The latter, strongly alter soundscape variations (Pijanowski et al. 2011). Alternatively, invasive species introduce novel noises, increasing diversity of the soundscape. As anthropogenic noise, they can be frequent, or of very long duration, or both (Hopkins et al. 2022). Invasive call can be loud (Farina et al. 2013), overlap in sound properties, match acoustic temporal periods with native bird calling [Azar et Bell (2016)](Hunt et al. 2024). Moreover, invasive predator can alter entire avian community and their biophony, like the invasive brown tree snake that is responsible for Guam’s “silent forests” (H. Rogers et al. 2017).

Although most of the world’s biodiversity is concentrated in tropical areas, habitat change impact on soundscape have mostly been studied in temperate contexts (Scarpelli et al. 2020) (Sugai et al. 2019) (Deichmann et al. 2018). Recent studies have pointed out the importance of invasive species as an under appreciated source of novel noise, that could alter bioacoustic components among native species (Hopkins et al. 2022). Still, current knowledge of invasive species effect on the soundscape is scarce and mostly focused on one or a few species at a time (see (Azar et Bell 2016)). These effects are even much less established in tropical island (Turlington et al. 2024). Despite being strongly threatened by human dominated areas and having important pool of mixed native and exotic avian species (Leclerc, Villéger, et al. 2020).

Parag 4: The knowledge gap

• Less considered is how habitat change and introduced birds combine do alter the soundscape • Why it matters: consequences for how humans perceive the soundscape (e.g., wellbeing, cultural value) and for how native birds perceive it (masking, acoustic niche, communication) • On islands where introduced species often largely dominate the bird community, at least in some habitats, how they reshape the soundscape is unclear

Within islands, diminished bird richness reflect fewer vocalizing species, resulting in less saturated soundscape than mainland (Robert et al. 2019). Acoustic interference are anticipated to lessened in insular systems. Yet, introduced species are numerous and are expected to increase in insular systems (Seebens et al. 2017). Introduced bird often dominate the acoustic environment they have invaded (Farina et al. 2013), personal observation from (Hopkins et al. 2022). Yet, their impact on soundscape are unclear and studies on native and exotic mixed avian community are limited [Azar et Bell (2016)](Hunt et al. 2024).

Therefore, habitat changes and biological invasions could have potential consequences for bird conspecific communication (Hopkins et al. 2022). In addition, bird biophony are also important for how people perceive exotic species and has strong implication for acoustic cultural heritage (Farina et Pieretti 2012). It is important to assess their impacts on insular soundscape, as these threats arise as combine effect in islands.

Parag 5: Study system and objectives

• Tahiti is an ideal place to address this: several valleys that serve as replicates, strong gradients of urbanisation (and altitude), and associated variation in the composition of both native and introduced bird communities • We captured this variation with our recorder deployment design, characterised the bird community using point counts, and extracted acoustic indices at each site • Objective: measure the effects of habitat and introduced birds on the soundscape using acoustic indices • Prediction: we expected invasive birds to occupy a significant part of the biophonic soundscape overall, and especially in altered habitats, with an opposite pattern for native species, which contribution to the soundscape may even be negligible or undetectable

Tahiti is an ideal place to address the question. With a strong habitat gradients, limited native avian communities and numerous introduced bird species, it allows to explore habitat alteration and introduced birds influence on the soundscape. Today, seven terrestrial native bird species remains and 12 introduced bird species have been introduced successfully (J.-C. Thibault et Cibois 2017). There is a noticeable spatial variation in the abundance and composition of introduced and native species communities across the island.

Here, across valleys with elevation gradients that serves as replicates, we sought to investigate the effects of habitat modification and bird assemblage gradients on the global soundscape and bird biophony of Tahiti. Using passive acoustic recorders, we characterized the variation of bird community composition from audio point counts, and extracted acoustic indices to describe soundscape variation. We expected introduced birds to occupy a significant part of bird biophony overall, especially in altered habitats, while human-dominated habitat have major implication of global soundscape [Fairbrass et al. (2017)](Hopkins et al. 2022). Conversely, we predicted an opposite pattern for native species, which contribution to bird biophony may even be negligible or undetectable.

2 Methods

Study area

We conducted the study in Tahiti, the largest (1,042 km²) and highest (2241 m) island of French Polynesia. The climate is tropical with an average annual minimum temperature of 23.5 °C and maximum of 30.3 °C, with an annual rainfall average of 1578.8 mm on the western coast (Faaa) and 3234.7 mm on the eastern part of the island (Tapearu-est) (Météo-France). Tahiti as other island of the Pacific ocean, underwent widespread stress on its terrestrial systems following first human colonization around the 10 th century (Kahn et al. 2015; Stevenson et al. 2017) and accentuated with Europeans arrival from the 18 th to the 19 th century (Pearson 1970). Still, peak habitat changes occurred from 1960 to 1990, due to development, coastal and lowland urbanization and forest clearance around estuaries (Bon 2005). Urban surfaces in Tahiti reached 30% in 2005, mainly concentrated on the coastline, but extended and gained heights since (DIREN, 2019).

Three main habitat types are distinguished in Tahiti. First, Tahiti is highly urbanized along the coastline, thus certain areas are much more developed than others, especially with expansive urbanisation around Papeete in the North-North West. Second, mixed sub-urban and open areas with moderate built-up, open vegetation located in the limits of expanding zone of the coastline and heights. Complemented by some agriculture, including cattle fields and horticulture on the plateaus of the peninsula and crop cultivation in the lowlands and large plains areas of Tahiti’s West and East coast. Then, a mostly forested inland, shaped by valleys with an interior difficult to access and elevated, though largely invaded by introduced plants (Meyer et Florence 1996) (Larrue et al. 2016).

Colonization of the island lead to hunting, habitat alteration and predator-competitor introduction, responsible for bird impoverishment and extinctions [Diamond (1989)](Leclerc, Villéger, et al. 2020). Data on extent of extinction are scarce, (Steadman 1995) estimated from bones that on average, ten species of seabirds and landbirds combined have been lost per Oceania’s major island. In Tahiti, four species are known to be extinct since the arrival of humans, 2 rails, 1 sandpiper and 1 parakeet species (birdsof2026?). Nowadays, Tahiti has 19 land bird species, with 12 of them that are introduced (J.-C. Thibault et Cibois 2017). Non-native birds have their highest densities in lowland, non-forested habitats such as urban areas or agricultural fields, and lower densities in high-elevation, contiguous forest, where native birds seem to have found refuge (Monnet et al. 1993) (J.-C. Thibault et Cibois 2017) (M. Thibault, Vidal, et al. 2018b).

Acoustic data collection

We collected passive acoustic data using SongMeter SM2 and SM4 microphones (Wildlife Acoustics, Maynard, MA, USA) across XX 2024 - XX 2026 period. In total, 76 sites of different habitat types ( Forest = , … open rural, suburban = , .. Urban = ) were surveyed along attitudinal gradients in valleys.

We placed acoustic recording units (ARUs) with minimal 200 meter distance between each-other. Devices were placed through semi-random placement depending on accessibility and minimal discretion. ARUs were programmed to record continuously and placed at 1,5 meters above ground. Devices recorded in average “…” per station, with a total of “…” hours recorded. Recordings were made as .WAV files with a “…” Hz sampling rate and gain of “…” dB, with monophonic and 16 Bits format.

Habitat metrics

We aimed at characterizing variation in both urbanization and habitat openness (to distinguish between open and forested habitats) across sites. To that aim, we extracted two metrics from satellite images available in Google Earth (Google Earth Pro.7.3.7.1327; images dated from 2023 and 2025, © Airbus). The Hard Surface Area (HSA) measures the percentage (from 0 to 100%) of hard surfaces (buildings, roads) in a given area and was used as a proxy for urbanization; the Herbaceous Cover (HERB) measures the percentage (from 0 to 100%) of open herbaceous areas. We measured HSA and HERB by drawing polygons around each habitat patch (hard surfaces, open herbaceous and forested) in 100 meter radius circles around each ARUs. The 100m radius scale size was selected to reflect local site urbanization and openness. While reflecting the main habitat types in which detected land bird vocalization or anthropic sounds find their sources.

Soundscape index metrics

We selected nine acoustic indices to encompass the sonic emissions of Tahiti. We based indices choice on their relevance to reflect the variability, heterogeneity and complexity of the global soundscape and the bird biophony for each sites. The multi-index approach was prioritized as it has been shown to be more effective than single index investigations (Bradfer-Lawrence et al. 2020). We estimated each acoustic indices for 10-min period and used hourly indices median value for better control of index variation (Bradfer-Lawrence et al. 2019). We normalized outputs to facilitate comparisons.

We estimated acoustic indices for two upper frequency limits, one in order to reflect the global soundscape and the second for bird biophony. While we applied a low frequency threshold for devices glitches. For the global soundscape, the upper limit was (… Hz) and we included recording containing rainfall to encompass all sonic emissions of the environment. For bird biophony the upper limit was (… Hz) and we filtered recordings containing rainfall sounds to focus solely on birds sonic emissions. We identified rainfall recordings with hardrains package in R (Metcalf et al. 2020). All indices were estimated using the default settings with ‘soundecology’ (…) and seewave (…) packages in R (..).

Bird community characterisation

We conducted two ten minutes audio point count in acoustic recordings from 06:00 to 06:10 am. We carried out identifications in recordings free of rainfall, wind or consistant-strong anthropogenic disturbance. If we detected acoustic perturbation, the next day was used instead and so on. We merged bird species detected in the two recording per site to obtain four bird assemblage metric : the total richness (count), introduced richness (count), presence of native bird (binomial) and presence of Common Myna (Acridotheres tristis) (binomial). We considered the presence/absence of native species rather than their richness since their richness varied between 0 and XX, with only XX sites where more than 2 natives species were detected. Variation in bird community structure across habitats

We first aimed at characterizing the effect of habitat and elevation on bird communities. To that aim, we built linear mixed-effect models to test whether the total richness and non-native richness (used as sequential response variables), were predicted by our two habitat variables (HSA and HERB). We also included altitude as covariable. We built generalized linear mixed-effect models for presence of native birds species or presence of common mynas with the same predictive variables. For count variable we used a XX distribution and a binomial distribution with a logit function for presence-absence response variable. Each model included a random fixed effect for the commune. All models were made using the “GlmmTMB” package (Brooks et al. 2017).

Variation in acoustic indices according to bird community structure and habitat

Prior to analysis, acoustic indices output were tested for correlations.

To investigate whether acoustic indices varied according to the bird community composition, we used GLMER to model each acoustic index (…) as response variable. That, to test whether acoustic indices outputs at both the global soundscape and bird biophony were predicted by one of the bird assemblage and our two habitat gradients. All models included a control fixed effect for the time (hour of the day) to account for temporal autocorrelation and random fixed effects for the site and commune. For XX and XX indices outputs we used a “…” distribution with a “…” function. All statistical analyses were performed in R v.().
